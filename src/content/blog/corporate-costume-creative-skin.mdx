There's a version of yourself you perform at work.

You know the one. It speaks in measured tones, defers to consensus, rounds off every sharp edge before it reaches the room. It uses words like "alignment" and "stakeholder" with a straight face. It wears the costume so well that some mornings you forget it's a costume at all.

I wore mine for years. Dark suits, careful slides, language scrubbed of anything that might make someone uncomfortable. I told myself it was professionalism. That this was how serious people operated in serious rooms.

It wasn't professionalism. It was fear dressed up in a blazer.

## The Costume Starts Early

Most of us put it on before we know we're doing it.

Someone laughs at your idea in a meeting. You learn to test ideas privately before sharing them publicly. Someone calls your design "too much" — and you file that phrase somewhere in your body, where it calcifies into instinct. Years later, you're making yourself smaller before anyone even asks you to.

The corporate costume isn't malicious. It's adaptive. It keeps you safe in environments that punish difference. But there's a cost no one tells you about when you first button it up: conformity is contagious, and it spreads from your external presentation inward.

You stop dressing differently. Then you stop thinking differently. Then you look up one day and realize the gap between what you show and what you believe has become so wide that you can't remember what you actually believed in the first place.

## What It Costs the Work

Here's what I noticed, sitting in rooms full of beautifully dressed people presenting beautifully sanitized slides:

The work looked the same.

Every deck had the same three-column layout, the same blue palette borrowed from nobody in particular, the same abstract imagery that communicated nothing. Every narrative hit the same beats in the same order. Every insight landed with the same muffled thud.

Not because the people making these decks were untalented. Most were brilliant. But they had learned — correctly, for their environments — that distinction was a liability. So they produced work that couldn't be criticized because it couldn't be identified. Safe, invisible, forgettable.

The problem is that forgettable doesn't move rooms. It doesn't close deals, secure funding, or shift how an executive understands a problem. Forgettable is expensive. It just bills itself as prudent.

Originality isn't decoration — it's a signal. When a presentation has a genuine point of view, people feel it immediately. They lean in. They engage differently. They remember it two weeks later when the decision is actually made.

Strip the point of view, and you strip the persuasion.

## The Myth of Safety

We believe that playing it safe protects us from criticism. And in a narrow sense, it does.

No one gets fired for the slide that looks like every other slide. No one gets laughed at for the idea that sounds like the last three ideas. The costume earns a kind of low-grade approval — not enthusiasm, not respect, but the quiet nod of people who recognize their own reflection.

What the costume doesn't protect against is irrelevance.

Irrelevance is the slow-moving risk that nobody tracks because it doesn't show up in a single meeting. It accumulates. The creative who always hedges becomes the creative nobody asks for audacious briefs. The designer who always defaults becomes the designer nobody calls when something actually matters. The executive who speaks in consensus becomes the executive nobody listens to when the room needs a real point of view.

Safety is a short-term hedge against a long-term ruin.

## Authenticity Isn't Bravery. It's Craft.

I want to be careful here, because "be authentic" has become the kind of advice that sounds meaningful and does nothing.

What I mean by authenticity isn't radical self-expression or posting unfiltered opinions or showing up to client meetings in your pajamas. It's something more precise and more demanding: it's the discipline of making choices that reflect genuine judgment rather than anticipated approval.

It's asking yourself, before every slide and every sentence and every design decision: *Is this what I actually think is right, or is this what I think they want to see?*

Those two things are often the same. But when they're not — that gap is where the costume lives.

The most effective communicators I know don't ignore what the room needs. They listen carefully. But they also have something to say that the room didn't know it needed, and they say it with enough conviction that the room reconsidered.

That's not bravery. That's craft. The craft of having developed a genuine perspective through enough experience and enough failure that you've earned the right to a point of view.

## The Slide That Changed My Mind

Years ago, I was designing a pitch deck for a healthcare startup. The brief was clear: authoritative, conservative, "enterprise-ready." The founders wanted something that looked like it belonged in a boardroom at Johnson & Johnson.

I designed exactly that. Eight slides in, it looked like every other healthcare deck I'd ever seen. Which, I realized, was the problem. If it looked like everything else, it would be remembered like everything else: briefly, and then not at all.

I took the costume off for a moment. I thought about what I actually believed about this company, this technology, this moment in healthcare. And then I designed what that belief looked like.

Different typeface. Different structure. Tighter copy, sharper claims, a color decision that had a reason. One slide that broke the grid deliberately, because the idea on it deserved the visual interruption.

The founders panicked slightly. It was "different" from what they'd asked for.

It was also the version that raised their round.

I'm not saying the design won the funding. The company's fundamentals won the funding. But the deck did something the original version couldn't: it communicated a genuine conviction. And in a room full of founders all claiming to have the answer, conviction reads.

## Putting the Costume Down

I'm not advocating for recklessness. Audiences are real, contexts matter, and some rooms genuinely require a particular register. A board deck for a Fortune 100 company operates differently than a pitch deck for a Series A startup.

But within every context, there's a choice. Between the safest possible interpretation and the most honest one. Between the design that will go unremarked and the design that will be remembered. Between the sentence that covers all the bases and the sentence that lands.

The costume won't protect you from that choice. It'll just make you forget you're making it.

The version of yourself that does the best work isn't the one who learned to disappear. It's the one who developed enough craft, enough judgment, and enough conviction to show up with something to say — and say it without apologizing in advance.

That version doesn't need a costume. They're already wearing their own skin.

And that, as it turns out, fits better than anything else.
