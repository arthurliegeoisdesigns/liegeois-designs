There was no moment.

That's the part I want to correct, because the story is usually told the other way round. A designer works for years making things look good, then one day something breaks open and they finally understand persuasion. It makes a tidy arc. It isn't what happened.

Wanting things to be beautiful was never something I acquired. It has always been there, in how I read a room, a page, a sentence. What changed wasn't the eye. It was everything I stacked behind it.

## Two Things That Rarely Sit Together

I studied business management before I ever designed a slide professionally. Then I spent years inside companies that take communication seriously. Oracle, where I carried a global account book. Apple, where I built and delivered presentations for executive audiences.

Neither of those was a design education. Both were something more useful.

I learned what a room full of executives is actually listening for. I learned that a decision has usually been half made before the deck opens, and the deck's job is to give people a reason to finish making it. I learned what a weak argument sounds like when someone with a budget is picking it apart in real time.

Most designers never get that. They learn composition, hierarchy, restraint, all of it real craft, and then they're handed someone else's argument and asked to make it look convincing. They can only work on the surface, because the surface is the only part they were trained to touch.

I came at it from the other side. I understood the argument first.

## The Switch Was Not Easy

Going from a corporate seat to working for myself was harder than I expected. The structure disappears. So does the assumption that you belong in the room.

What surprised me was how quickly clients noticed I was picking things up. Not the design, which they expected, but the business underneath it. I'd ask about the deal, the buying committee, the objection they were dreading. They'd answer, then pause, because their designer wasn't supposed to ask that.

## RAPP

One of my first clients was RAPP.

I had never worked at an agency before. Agencies have their own metabolism, their own shorthand, and a low tolerance for people who slow it down. I expected to spend months proving I could keep up.

Instead, I blended in fast, and started delivering sales pitches. The ones agencies use to win accounts. They won work.

Then RAPP began positioning me across other entities in the Omnicom group.

That's the part that told me something. Not a client saying the slides looked good. Clients say that to be nice. An agency that had every reason to keep a useful resource to itself was instead recommending me sideways, to people whose opinion of them depended on whether I delivered. Nobody does that for pretty.

## What I Actually Do

The method is less glamorous than the result.

**I listen first.** Not politely, waiting for my turn. Proactively listening, to understand the big picture. It's amazing what you can discover when you are the quietest person in the room. And then I start connecting the dots, identifying the bottlenecks and the pain. Whether my client is a creative agency, a VC or a founder, the stakes are high, and so is the pressure.

**Then I ask questions.** Innocent ones first. "What does this word mean?" "Who is your audience?" "What are their expectations?" "What happens if they say no?" Wiping the fog off the bathroom mirror. Then come the challenging ones. The ones that earn the fee: "What are you trying to say?" "Do you think your audience wants to see a table full of numbers they can't even read?" "What would your sharpest competitor say to this claim?" "If they only remember one thing, what would it be?"

**Then we strip the meat from the bone.** This is the part clients don't expect and, eventually, the part they value most. We keep cutting until what's left is the actual argument. The irreducible thing they need this room to accept. "How many stakeholders dumped their slides into this deck?" "Did you streamline the narrative after they did?" And my favourite: "Did you write your narrative as a script first, or did you jump straight into the slides?"

Let's be clear. My process isn't perfect. It's organic, constantly adapting to what clients say, or don't say. It's iterative. Brainstorming mode. Sometimes rough around the edges. French, too (ha, did I lose you there?). Not the arrogant kind. Just direct, sharing thoughts and constructive feedback built on real business acumen.

At this stage, one of two things happens.

Either they realise I'm not their usual designer, and they take the feedback. It's almost always here that someone says the quiet part, usually not to me: *this is why our last deck didn't work.* Not the layout. Not the template. The narrative wasn't clear. It didn't land because we didn't match what our audience wanted to see and hear. We never streamlined it.

Or, rarely, they push back. "We hired you to make things pretty. Do your job." That's usually when I decide whether I want my name on their deck, or whether we part ways.

Assuming it's the first, that's when production starts.

## Understood Is Not Undeniable

Understanding is cognitive. I often hear the expression "did the message land?" I prefer "did I succeed in bonding with my audience?"

I'll be straightforward. I have watched a lot of presenters spend their time talking about themselves. Bragging a little. Explaining the features of their next big product, or their revolutionary service. Missing the point.

Bonding with your audience is not about you. It's about them. Your audience doesn't care about features. They care about what those features do for them. And they want one question answered the moment they walk in: *what's in it for me?*

So I tell clients the same thing. My job isn't to make your slides pretty. It's to build a deck that helps you bond with your audience, earn their trust and bring them on board, by immersing them in the journey you have prepared for them. My ONLY job is to support your speech, at the right pace, so you can focus on them and not on your speaker notes. So you can deliver your narrative seamlessly, while your slides complement you.

When that big number appears on screen at the very moment you say it. When the visual lands exactly when you need it, and it makes them laugh. Small tricks, big wins.

That's how the experience lingers long after they leave. Unforgettable, convincing, undeniable.

## Narrative First. Slides Second.

Information is the raw material. Design is the engine. But the destination is always a decision, and decisions are made on conviction, then justified with logic afterwards.

So the question was never *how do I design this slide.*

It's this. What does this room need to believe by the time they leave, what is the shortest honest path to that belief, and what on this slide is standing in the way?

Everything else is decoration.

Strip it back. Cut to the bone. Find the argument. Then build.
